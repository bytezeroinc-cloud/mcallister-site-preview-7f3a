    Restraining Orders in California: A Clear Guide for the Person Named in One

    EPO, CPO, DVRO — the alphabet arrives fast and the consequences are immediate. What each order does, how they differ, and what can actually be changed.

    Free case review →→ (310) 881-2786 Answered 24/7 · no screening service

    Most guides about restraining orders are written for the person asking for one. This one is for the person the envelope is addressed to — because if that's you, you have obligations that started before you understood them, and options nobody has explained.

    First, the rule that keeps people out of new trouble: whatever you think of the order, follow it to the letter while it exists. Violating a protective order is its own crime under Penal Code 273.6 — a reconciling text counts, and an invitation from the protected person is not a defense. The order binds you, not them.

    The three orders, untangled

    An Emergency Protective Order is what police request at the scene of a domestic call — a judge signs it by phone, day or night, and it lasts up to seven days. It's designed to be fast, which means it's issued before anyone has heard your side.

    A Criminal Protective Order is issued by the criminal court at arraignment if charges are filed, and it typically runs as long as the case does — longer on conviction.

    A Domestic Violence Restraining Order is a civil order the other person requests in family court, with its own hearing and its own life span — up to five years, renewable. It exists whether or not criminal charges are ever filed.

    They can all exist at once, they don't automatically end together, and each has its own path to modification.

    The part nobody tells you: orders have settings

    "Protective order" sounds binary — you're either ordered away or you're not. In reality, California orders come in levels. A full stay-away order can, with the right showing, become a "peaceful contact" order that lets a family live under one roof while a case is pending. Judges modify orders regularly — for housing, for children, for work — but only when someone asks, prepares the request and supports it.

    The first hearing is where this happens, and it comes fast. Walking in unprepared usually means the broadest version of the order continues by default. Walking in with counsel, a housing plan, and — where they support it — the protected person's own position, is a different hearing entirely.

    Children, work and the record

    A protective order shapes custody long before any family-court judgment: exchanges, school pickups, whose home the children sleep in. Getting the order's terms right protects those routines while the case is decided.

    Work matters too — some orders reach workplaces and shared professional spaces, and licensing boards ask about them. And while a restraining order is not a conviction, DVROs affect firearm rights immediately and appear in background systems while active. Every one of these consequences is an argument for treating the order hearing as seriously as the criminal case.

    What we do in week one

    Read the order — the actual text, because obligations vary. Calendar every hearing. Preserve the messages and the 911 audio while they exist. Prepare the modification request that fits your life — housing, children, work. And if criminal charges haven't been filed yet, open the prefile conversation with the reviewing attorney while the decision is still open.

    An order that arrived overnight can feel permanent. Very little about it is — but none of it changes by itself.

    Follow it, then fight itCompliance and challenge are not opposites. You obey the order that exists today and argue for the order that should exist next month — that's how it's done safely.

    Questions we hear on this

    01The protected person wants to see me. Can we just meet?

    Not while a no-contact order exists. Their invitation is not a legal defense — the order binds you. The lawful route is a modification request, which courts grant regularly where it's safe and supported.

    02Does a restraining order give me a criminal record?

    The order itself is not a conviction. But violating it is a crime, and active orders appear in law-enforcement systems and affect firearm rights. Treat the hearing seriously and the record question stays manageable.
