    Three Strikes in California: What Counts as a Strike — and What It Does to a Case

    “Strike” is the scariest word in a California felony file. Here's precisely what it means, the arithmetic behind it, and the three separate ways it gets fought.

    Free case review →→ (310) 881-2786 Answered 24/7 · no screening service

    The word arrives casually — a line in the complaint, a comment from a bailiff — and it changes the temperature of everything: they're calling it a strike.

    Here's the precise version. California's Three Strikes law isn't about three chances at anything. It's a sentencing structure that attaches to specific convictions — "serious" felonies listed in Penal Code 1192.7 and "violent" felonies in 667.5 — and changes the math of every case that comes after them.

    The actual arithmetic

    One prior strike doubles the sentence range on a new felony and caps custody credits — you serve more of a longer sentence. Two prior strikes, and a new serious or violent felony exposes you to 25-to-life.

    The list of strike offenses includes the ones you'd guess — robbery, residential burglary, assault with a deadly weapon causing great bodily injury — and some you might not. It also excludes more than people assume: most drug offenses, most theft, most DUIs. Whether the charge in front of you is a strike is a legal question with a specific answer, not a vibe.

    Three separate fights

    A strike allegation is contested in three distinct ways, and a serious defense runs all three.

    Is it actually a strike? Priors must be pled and proven from the record of conviction. Old records are incomplete, out-of-state priors don't map cleanly onto California's list, and wobbler priors reduced to misdemeanors don't count. Allegations fail on their paperwork more often than advertised.

    Should the current charge be a strike? Many charged offenses have non-strike alternatives a case can resolve to — the difference between 245(a)(1) with a GBI enhancement and without it is the difference between a strike and not.

    Should the court strike the strike? Under Romero, judges can dismiss strike priors "in the interests of justice" — age of the prior, its facts, the life lived since. A Romero motion isn't a form; it's a biography, built deliberately.

    What this means for your case today

    Strike arithmetic changes early decisions most of all. Bail arguments, whether the preliminary hearing is litigated or waived (litigated — nearly always), which counts a negotiation should target first, whether a plea today creates the strike that doubles a sentence in a case you can't foresee.

    That last one deserves its own sentence: the most expensive strikes are the ones accepted casually in quick pleas, years before they mattered.

    If a strike is alleged against you

    Get the record of every alleged prior — the actual documents. Litigate the prelim. Build the Romero record from day one, not the week before sentencing. And make every resolution decision with the next ten years in the room, not just the next ten weeks.

    A strike allegation is arithmetic, and arithmetic argues. The first conversation about how is free.

    Pled and provenA strike prior isn't a fact until the People prove it from the record of conviction. Incomplete records, out-of-state priors and reduced wobblers all fail that test regularly.

    Questions we hear on this

    01Is my old conviction a strike?

    It depends on the statute of conviction, the plea's specific admissions, and sometimes conduct findings in the record. This is answerable from documents — and worth answering before any new case is resolved.

    02Can a strike ever come off my record?

    The prior conviction itself may be reducible or dismissible depending on what it was, and in a current case a judge can strike the prior for sentencing under Romero. Different tools, different standards — both worth mapping.
