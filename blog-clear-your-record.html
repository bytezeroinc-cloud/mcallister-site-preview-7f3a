    Expungement in California: Clearing Your Record with 1203.4, 17(b) and Sealing

    The law on old convictions has widened repeatedly — and almost nobody was notified. A practical map of the petitions that change what a background check sees.

    Free case review →→ (213) 587-4631 Answered 24/7 · no screening service

    Every week we meet someone carrying a conviction that stopped being permanent years ago — they were just never told.

    California's record-relief law isn't one thing; it's a toolkit, and the right tool depends on what happened and when. Here's the honest map, in plain English.

    PC 1203.4 — the dismissal most people call “expungement”

    If you completed probation (or your sentence, under newer provisions) on an eligible case, Penal Code 1203.4 lets the court withdraw the conviction and dismiss the case. Your record then shows the dismissal — and for most private-sector job applications, you may lawfully answer as though the conviction doesn't exist.

    Honesty about its limits: it isn't erasure. Licensing boards and government applications see the history; certain consequences survive. But for the background checks that decide apartments and paychecks, a 1203.4 dismissal changes lives, and courts grant them every day.

    PC 17(b) — the felony that becomes a misdemeanor

    If your felony was a wobbler — an offense chargeable either way, like many theft and assault counts — and probation was granted, 17(b) lets the court reduce it to a misdemeanor. For all purposes, going forward. "Have you been convicted of a felony?" becomes a different question.

    There's no deadline. We've reduced wobblers a decade after probation ended. Pair it with 1203.4 and an old felony becomes a dismissed misdemeanor — which is usually the strongest version of the past available.

    Sealing, and the relief nobody applies for

    Arrests that never became convictions — charges declined, cases dismissed, acquittals — can be sealed under PC 851.91, taking the arrest record itself out of most background systems. People carry declined arrests on their records for years without knowing this exists.

    Beyond these, the last decade of reform keeps applying retroactively: Prop 47 reclassified many drug and theft felonies to misdemeanors; Prop 64 did the same for cannabis, with courts required to act; newer automatic-relief laws clear eligible records by operation of law — eventually, and imperfectly. "Eventually and imperfectly" is why checking your actual RAP sheet beats waiting.

    How this works in practice

    We pull the record and read it — county by county, case by case, because eligibility lives in the details: what the conviction was, what probation looked like, what's still open. Then the petitions are filed in the right order, because sequencing matters: reduce, then dismiss, then seal what qualifies.

    Most petitions resolve without you appearing. The paperwork is unglamorous. The result — a background check that finally matches who you've been for the last ten years — is not.

    Bring us a case number, or even just a county and an approximate year. The eligibility answer is usually available within days, and the first conversation is free.

    No deadline17(b) reductions and 1203.4 dismissals have no filing deadline. A conviction from 2012 is exactly as petitionable this year as it was the year probation ended.

    Questions we hear on this

    01Will clearing my record restore my gun rights?

    Not by itself — 1203.4 expressly doesn't restore firearm rights, and some convictions carry their own prohibitions. Firearm-rights analysis is separate, and honest counsel keeps it separate.

    02Do I qualify if I went to prison instead of probation?

    1203.4 historically required probation, but newer provisions (including PC 1203.41 and 1203.425's automatic relief) reach some sentences that ended in custody. Translation: don't self-disqualify — the answer is in the docket, not the rumor.
